\paragraph{Key observation.}
Kubernetes maintained 100\,\% success rate across all tested levels (L1--L6) with stable execution times.
Mean execution time increased modestly from 63.4\,s (L1) to 81.6\,s (L6), a relative increase of $\approx$29\,\%.
The standard deviation at L5 (9.6\,s) and L6 (8.0\,s) is higher than at lower levels, reflecting mild aggregate memory pressure at 7--10 concurrent pods on the 8\,GB node, but remaining well within the pod's 2\,GiB limit.
Execution time variance remained low and stable across all levels, confirming that pod-level resource limits effectively shielded jobs from inter-job contention throughout the tested concurrency range.

\subsection{Part B --- Blast Radius Test}
\label{subsec:results-exp2b}

The blast radius test was conducted at level L4 (5 concurrent jobs, 3 repetitions per environment).
Rather than active failure injection, this test measures whether the resource-contention failures that occur organically under concurrent load on the VM cascade to sibling jobs --- and whether Kubernetes pod isolation prevents that cascading.
Both environments ran the same \texttt{thesis\_workload} at L4 and job outcomes were recorded.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{blast_radius.png}
  \caption{Blast radius test at L4 (5 concurrent jobs): job success rate for VM vs.\ Kubernetes. VM resource-contention failures cascade to sibling jobs (40\,\% SR); Kubernetes pod isolation fully contains failures (100\,\% SR).}
  \label{fig:blast-radius}
\end{figure}

On the VM, shared-resource contention (memory pressure and CPU throttling across unbound containers) caused 60\,\% of jobs to fail when 5 ran concurrently.
Failed jobs exhausted the 4\,GB node RAM, creating system-level OOM conditions that terminated sibling containers --- blast radius = 1 (CASCADES).
On Kubernetes, each pod operates within its 2\,GiB memory limit enforced by the kernel cgroups hierarchy.
No failures were observed: all 15 jobs at L4 completed successfully --- blast radius = 0 (CONTAINED).
This confirms that Kubernetes pod isolation fully prevents cross-job failure propagation at this load level, directly answering SQ2.

\subsection{Part C --- Scheduling Overhead (SQ3 Supplementary)}
\label{subsec:results-exp2c}

Pod scheduling latency and container startup time were collected from Kubernetes pod event timestamps at each concurrency level (Table~\ref{tab:overhead-summary}).
These data directly address SQ3 and feed into the crossover analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results-exp3}.

\paragraph{Measurement methodology.}
Startup overhead is computed by joining \texttt{pod\_timing.csv} with \texttt{dagster\_runs.csv} on \texttt{run\_id}: startup\,overhead = total\,pod\,lifetime $-$ Dagster\,exec\,time.
Note: the \texttt{job\_start\_ts} field records the \texttt{ContainersReady} condition change on container \emph{exit}, so subtracting \texttt{submitted\_ts} gives total pod lifetime, not startup time.

At L1--L6, all pods were scheduled within $<$1\,s of submission and startup overhead ranged from 24.4\,s (L1) to 56.6\,s (L6), growing with concurrency as more containers compete for Python interpreter init and PostgreSQL handshake slots.
No scheduling timeouts or pending states were observed at any tested level (Table~\ref{tab:overhead-summary}).
